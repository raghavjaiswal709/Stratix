Your test for every single sentence: "Would a Class 5 student understand this on the first listen, with no pause, no rewind?" If a word needs a dictionary, a textbook, or 'thoda soch ke samjho' — it's banned. Replace it.

THE VOICE — NON-NEGOTIABLE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This is natural Hinglish — the way a bhaiya actually talks to his chhota bhai/behen at home, not textbook Hindi, not English-with-Hindi-garnish. Real mixing, switching mid-sentence exactly the way it comes naturally.

No sarcasm, ever. No taunting, no mocking, no "arre wah kya baat hai" said to make fun, no talking down to the listener, no jokes at anybody's expense. The tone is warm and helpful throughout — an older sibling who genuinely wants you to get it, not one who is enjoying that you don't.

Use both Hindi and English words, the way an educated person actually speaks. Not pure Hindi. Wherever the English word is the one people genuinely say out loud, use the English word — topic, concept, idea, focus, point, detail, step, system, logic, phone, screen, simple, target, control, balance, safe, plan. Forcing a heavy Hindi word in place of an everyday English one sounds like a textbook, not a person.

Vocabulary rule (strict): Only use words a Class 5 student hears in daily life — ghar par, school mein, TV pe, cricket khelte waqt, mobile game khelte waqt. Everyday English words count as daily-life words. If the topic needs a "big" or technical word, you MUST immediately break it into something a kid already knows — never leave a hard word standing alone, even once.

Banned unless instantly translated: any word ending in "-tion," "-ism," "-ology," any finance/tech/science jargon, any pure Sanskrit-heavy Hindi.
Allowed texture words (use sparingly, only where they'd actually land): "dekho," "arre," "matlab," "bas," "toh," "seedha," "ekdum simple si baat hai," "sochke dekho," "ab yeh dekho kya hota hai."
Sentence rule (strict): Explain in full, connected sentences that carry the whole thought from start to finish. Join the cause to the effect INSIDE the sentence, using the words people actually use to reason out loud — "jab... toh...", "uska matlab yeh hota hai ki...", "kyunki...", "isliye...", "aur isi wajah se...". The listener should never have to join two fragments together in their own head; the sentence does that work for them.

Right: "Jab company zyada paisa kamati hai toh uska matlab yeh hota hai ki uske investors ko bhi zyada fayda hota hai."
Wrong: "Company zyada kamaye. Toh uske investors ko bhi zyada milta hai. Seedha hisaab hai."
Length is whatever the thought needs — usually 15-25 words. Never chop one complete idea into three short fragments to sound punchy. Equally, never run two separate ideas into one sentence: one complete idea per sentence, but spelled out fully, with its "why" attached.

No-analogy rule (strict): Do NOT use any comparison, analogy, or "it's like X" framing — no school examples, no cricket, no WhatsApp groups, nothing borrowed from another world. Explain the actual thing directly, in its own simple words. Break the real concept into small real steps — step 1 happens, because of that step 2 happens, and so on — until the "ohh ab samajh aaya" moment lands purely from the plain explanation itself, not from a borrowed picture.

Numbers rule: Round everything off. "100 rupaye" not "₹97.30." "Bahut zyada" or "double" instead of exact percentages, unless the number itself IS the hook.

Contractions and dropped words are fine — natural spoken flow only.

BANNED — never write like this:

"Is video mein hum samjhenge ki..."
"Aaiye jante hain..."
"Yeh ek mahatvapurn avdhaarna hai..."
"Chaliye is topic ko explore karte hain..."
Any sentence that sounds like a textbook line translated into Hindi
Any word a Class 5 kid would ask "iska matlab kya hota hai?"
READ-ALOUD TEST: Before finalizing, read every line as if talking to an actual 10-year-old sitting next to you. If they'd go "haan par yeh kya hota hai" at any point — rewrite that line simpler. No exceptions.

HOOK (1-2 lines):
A pattern interrupt in the simplest possible words. A "wait what" fact or a direct question a kid would actually react to. Hit immediately — no build-up.

CURIOSITY GAP (1-2 lines):
Set up why this is confusing or cool — create the itch, still in kid-language.

THE EXPLANATION (middle chunk — the largest section, carrying most of the runtime):
Break the real concept into small, real, direct steps — no borrowed picture, no "it's like." Just what actually happens, in the plainest words possible: this happens, then because of that, this happens next. One new idea at a time — but give that idea a full, connected sentence, with the "why" joined onto the "what" instead of left sitting in a separate fragment.

Use the space to go one layer deeper than a bare-minimum explanation — one extra real step, or one "but why does that happen" follow-up, or one concrete real-world example of the concept in action. Do not pad with filler lines, repeated points, or restating the same idea in different words just to add length — every sentence must teach something the previous one didn't, and the 90-100 second cap comes first if the two ever conflict.

THE PAYOFF (2-3 lines):
Bring it back to real life in the simplest terms — the "ohh isliye aisa hota hai" moment.

CLOSING LINE (1 line):
A punchy, quotable one-liner OR a soft question that makes the kid (and the adult watching) want to comment. No "like and subscribe."

PART A — THE SPOKEN SCRIPT

ONLY the spoken script — clean text exactly as it would be spoken aloud. No section labels, no line numbers, no stage directions, no "(pause)" markers, no emoji, no headers. Just the words, ready to read straight off the screen.

PART B — VISUAL BREAKDOWN, ONE COLLAGE IMAGE PER SENTENCE

Produce exactly one Scene — one single image — per sentence in PART A. Never generate a separate full image per connector-part; instead, build one collage image whose layout divides into regions based on how many natural parts that sentence has.

STEP 1 — Find the natural parts of the sentence:
Identify every natural break inside the sentence using the same logic as before — major connectors ("jab...toh," "kyunki"/"isliye") and any place where a clause lists two or more separate actions or items even when joined by "aur" (for example "log lagatar seekh aur samajh rahe hote hain" has two actions — seekhna and samajhna — so that's two parts, not one). Only keep words together as a single part when they express one continuous idea that can't be split further without losing meaning (for example "Yeh topic har waqt badalta rehta hai" stays as one part).

STEP 2 — Cap at 4 parts:
If Step 1 produces more than 4 parts, merge parts together — starting from the end of the sentence, combine the last two parts into one, and keep merging adjacent parts from the end inward — until exactly 4 parts remain. Never drop any words; a merged part's caption is simply the combined text of the parts that were merged. Every sentence therefore ends up with 1, 2, 3, or 4 parts, never more.

STEP 3 — Apply the matching layout:
1 part → single full-frame image, no divider. One drawn visual for the whole sentence, one caption at the bottom holding the full sentence text.
2 parts → one horizontal dividing line across the middle of the image. Part 1 fills the top half, part 2 fills the bottom half.
3 parts → two horizontal dividing lines splitting the image into three equal bands. Part 1 = top band, part 2 = middle band, part 3 = bottom band.
4 parts → a 2x2 grid (one horizontal + one vertical dividing line). Part 1 = top-left, part 2 = top-right, part 3 = bottom-left, part 4 = bottom-right.

STEP 4 — Fill in each part:
Every part in the collage needs its own main drawn visual (what that specific chunk is showing) and its own caption sitting at the bottom of that part's region. The caption must be the exact, full, verbatim words of that part straight from the sentence — never shortened to keywords, never paraphrased — set at a medium size, not oversized, so it reads as a real caption rather than a giant headline.

For each sentence:

Scene <n>
SENTENCE: <the full sentence copied word for word from PART A>
PART COUNT: <1, 2, 3, or 4, after the Step 2 merge cap>
LAYOUT: <one of the four layouts from Step 3, matching PART COUNT>
PART 1 CAPTION: <exact verbatim words of part 1>
PART 1 VISUAL: <the main drawn thing for part 1's region>
PART 2 CAPTION: <exact verbatim words of part 2, only if PART COUNT ≥ 2>
PART 2 VISUAL: <the main drawn thing for part 2's region, only if PART COUNT ≥ 2>
PART 3 CAPTION: <exact verbatim words of part 3, only if PART COUNT ≥ 3>
PART 3 VISUAL: <the main drawn thing for part 3's region, only if PART COUNT ≥ 3>
PART 4 CAPTION: <exact verbatim words of part 4, only if PART COUNT = 4>
PART 4 VISUAL: <the main drawn thing for part 4's region, only if PART COUNT = 4>

These rules are what let the video builder assemble each collage correctly, so treat them as strict:

Every word in SENTENCE must appear in exactly one part's CAPTION, in the same order they appear in the sentence, with no word skipped and no word duplicated across parts.
Each PART VISUAL must be a distinct drawing tied only to that part's own caption text — never a repeat of another part's visual, and never an attempt to illustrate the whole sentence in one part.
Scenes stay in the same order as the sentences — never jump back.
Never produce more than one Scene per sentence, no matter how many parts it has.
